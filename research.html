01 / Materials synthesis · OSU & Purdue

Nanomaterial synthesis

I synthesize titanium carbide MXene (Ti₃C₂), an ultrathin two-dimensional material, using wet-chemical etching and delamination to produce single- to few-layer flakes.

At Purdue, I gained hands-on experience synthesizing MAX phases, a class of layered ceramic materials, as well as MXenes derived from them. My work involved powder processing, vertical milling, high-temperature sintering in a tube furnace, and wet-chemical etching.

My synthesis experience also includes defect-engineered Ti₃C₂ flakes, double-transition-metal MXenes, and high-entropy MXenes. Freeze-drying prepares the processed flakes for later formulation into nanofluids or inks.

Focus
Synthesis of layered ceramic materials and ultrathin two-dimensional flakes
Methods
Powder processing, sintering, wet-chemical etching, delamination, centrifugation, and freeze-drying

With Dr. Pranjal Nautiyal and Prof. Babak Anasori’s group

02 / Coating & thin-film manufacturing · OSU

Coating manufacturing

I investigate tribosintering as a way to manufacture MXene coatings directly at a sliding contact. In this process, contact pressure and sliding-induced stresses help consolidate material delivered in an MXene ink into a surface film.

Using a Mini Traction Machine (MTM), I study film formation between a ball and disc. In-situ optical interferometry uses reflected light to follow changes in film thickness as the coating forms, wears, and forms again.

Focus
Controlling MXene film formation as a coating manufacturing process
Methods
MXene ink formulation, MTM ball-on-disc processing, in-situ optical interferometry

Advisor: Dr. Pranjal Nautiyal · Extreme Interfaces Laboratory

Research overview

Producing continuous, durable MXene coatings is a central challenge. This research explores whether sliding contact can turn MXene ink into a bonded film at the interface.

Tracking film growth during processing helps connect contact conditions with coating development. The broader study aims to combine these observations with microscopy, spectroscopy, and mechanical characterization to understand how processing influences film structure and durability.

In-situ optical recording

Watching a tribofilm form

A tribofilm is a surface layer created during rubbing through tribochemical and mechanical interactions between dispersed nanoparticles, the base oil, and the metallic substrate. Localized shear stress, contact pressure, and frictional heat drive these interactions, building a film that can change how the surfaces interact.

This recording captures film growth, local removal, and renewed formation. Following these changes helps me study the balance between coating formation and wear.

03 / Nanomechanics · OSU / Purdue collaboration

AFM nanomechanics
& nanowear testing

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) combines precise force control with nanoscale surface imaging. I use it to investigate how individual MXene flakes respond to indentation and sliding, connecting their processing history with local deformation and wear.

Focus
Nanoscale mechanical behavior and wear resistance of ultrathin two-dimensional flakes
Methods
AFM nanoindentation, nanoscratch testing, surface topography mapping

With Dr. Pranjal Nautiyal and Prof. Babak Anasori’s group

Ta₄C₃ MXene · Local mechanical response

Nanoindentation &
residual depth analysis

I use a diamond AFM tip to indent Ta₄C₃ MXene flakes and measure the deformation left in the surface. Comparing flakes with different heat-treatment histories helps reveal how thermal processing influences their resistance to localized deformation.

The remaining indentation depth provides a way to compare the flakes’ mechanical response and assess how processing may affect their stability under contact loading.

AFM nanoindentation · Surface topography · Residual deformation

Ti₃C₂ MXene · Resistance to wear

Nanoscratch &
wear-depth analysis

I create controlled scratches on individual MXene flakes to examine their resistance to wear. Comparing flakes prepared under different etching conditions helps relate synthesis to their wear response.

AFM maps the surface topography before and after nanoscratch testing. Profiles across the resulting tracks measure wear depth at the nanoscale relative to the adjacent unworn flake surface.

AFM nanoscratch · Surface topography · Nanoscale wear depth

Test procedures

Instrument & calibration

Both studies use an MFP-3D AFM and an AD-40-AS diamond probe in ambient air at room temperature. Cantilever stiffness is calibrated by thermal noise, and deflection sensitivity from hard contact on a silicon reference.

Indentation & scratching

Indentation applies controlled normal loads. Nanoscratches are produced in AFM lithography mode at progressively higher loads. Surface topography is recorded to capture the remaining indentations and wear tracks.

Depth analysis

After leveling and row alignment, surface profiles measure the deepest point of each indentation or scratch relative to the surrounding undeformed flake surface. Raised material around the features is excluded from the reference surface.

04 / Undergraduate thesis · BUET

MoTe₂–graphene
heterostructures

I used molecular dynamics simulations in LAMMPS to study the mechanical properties of molybdenum ditelluride (MoTe₂) and MoTe₂–graphene heterostructures. Adding graphene increased the simulated stiffness and tensile strength to nearly 3.5 times those of MoTe₂ alone.

Heat-transport simulations of MoTe₂ showed that higher temperature and stretching reduced thermal conductivity along the sheets, while adding layers increased conductivity across them. These findings help guide the design of ultrathin materials for nanoelectronics and thermoelectric devices.

LAMMPS · Mechanical properties · Thermal transport

Supervisor: Dr. A. K. M. Monjur Morshed · BUET
